The network

Relationships I hold, and how they connect

These are working relationships, built in shared rooms over shared work, not a contact list.

At national level I have partnered with the Ministry of Education across its three state departments, Basic Education, Vocational and Technical Training, and Higher Education and Research, and with the agencies that carry the technical load: the Teachers Service Commission on teacher management, KICD on curriculum, KNEC on assessment, KEMI on school leadership, and CEMASTEA on STEM pedagogy.

That work cascades to the counties through their County Directors of Education, currently across 19: Samburu, Laikipia, Meru, Isiolo, Marsabit, Garissa, Lamu, Kilifi, Baringo, West Pokot, Narok, Elgeyo Marakwet, Nakuru, Kajiado, Machakos, Kisumu, Siaya, Homa Bay and Vihiga.

Delivery runs with partners who each hold a piece: UNICEF, the LEGO Foundation, Ubongo, the ICT Authority, KENIA, the Kenya Association of Innovation Hubs, EdTech East Africa and Human Practice Foundation.

How I built them

Built in shared rooms, over shared work

Every one of these started while doing the work, not while asking for a meeting. That is why the evidence I bring later gets a hearing.

01

I sat in the policy reviews

Contributing to Foundational Literacy and Numeracy policy reviews means the officials who set the standard know my evidence and my face, not just my organisation's name on a report.

02

I co-facilitated the summits

Co-facilitating regional and Central Kenya EdTech summits put me on the same side of the table as county and national officials, building a shared position rather than lobbying for one.

03

I judged and mentored on the ground

Judging the World Robot Olympiad across two seasons built trust with CEMASTEA and county STEM officers, because they watched me do the work with their own students.

04

I ran the teacher townhalls

Facilitating Teachers' Townhalls moved teachers from audience to co-authors of policy. When the recommendations went up, the people who delivered them were the ones defending them.

Rural digital access argued on a national stage, not just delivered in the field.

Scaling through partnership

From an ASAL pilot to a national policy line

A worked example of using these relationships to grow a programme: evidence-based EdTech carried from regional conversations into national ones, and into reviews and updates of national education policy.

  1. Pilot where it is hardest

    We integrated ICT into learning in ASAL schools across the northern and Rift counties. Offline-first, teacher-led, measured from day one so the results would stand up.

  2. Bring the County Directors in early

    County Directors of Education co-owned the rollout, so the outcomes were theirs to defend rather than mine to sell.

  3. Turn evidence into a shared regional position

    At regional EdTech conversations we set the ASAL data beside other counties' and agreed what it meant together, with UNICEF, the LEGO Foundation and EdTech East Africa in the room.

  4. Carry it into the national review

    That shared position fed evidence-based EdTech into national conversations and into reviews and updates of national education policy, including on Foundational Literacy and Numeracy.

A programme that started in a handful of ASAL classrooms is now argued for at national level, by the officials who run those classrooms.

Inclusion in practice

Underserved is a design brief, not a disclaimer

The pioneer HPF ICT Academy cohort in Maasai Mara was built for people the mainstream pipeline skips: out-of-school youth and teen mothers in a pastoralist county.

We designed around their lives. Childcare-aware timetables. A laptop each to keep and earn on. Mother tongue and Kiswahili alongside English. One-to-one coaching so nobody stayed stuck at their own screen. That cohort of 26 closed at a 95% pass rate and 86% completion, and graduates are earning verified income on digital work platforms.
